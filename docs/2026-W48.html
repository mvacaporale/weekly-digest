Weekly Digest — Nov 21 – Nov 27, 2026

The Solomon Islands traded its Chinese security pact for Australia, Baghdad took over Iraqi Kurdistan's oil fields and borders, and the UN climate talks ended with no deal at all. In AI, Washington's hand on compute got heavier (SpaceX's million-satellite orbital data centers cleared, Nvidia's board brought Jensen Huang back), and agents got permission to approve code and patch live servers.

The World

1. China just lost its main security foothold in the South Pacific. The Solomon Islands dropped its Chinese security pact and named Australia its "primary security partner."

The background you need: The Solomon Islands is a chain of islands about 2,000 km northeast of Australia, on the sea routes between Australia, Asia and the US. Guadalcanal, one of those islands, was a key WWII battleground for exactly that reason. In 2022 the government signed a secret security pact with China that allowed Chinese police and possibly naval visits. Washington and Canberra treated it as the first step toward a Chinese military presence in their backyard, and it set off a scramble for influence across the Pacific islands.

What happened: Prime Minister Matthew Wale ended the 2022 China pact and signed a treaty with Australian PM Anthony Albanese that makes Australia the country's "primary security partner" (Nov 25). The same week, China and Cambodia began their largest-ever joint military exercise, Golden Dragon 2026, with nearly 3,000 troops (Nov 25). So Beijing's regional push hasn't stopped, but it just lost its showcase partner in the Pacific.

Why you'll care later: The 2022 pact was the textbook example of China expanding its security reach into the Pacific. This treaty reverses it in writing, and other island states weighing Beijing's offers will treat it as precedent. In the long arc of US–China competition, this is control of a strategic position changing hands without a shot fired.

Thread to pull: What did China's 2022 security pact with the Solomon Islands actually permit, why did the Wale government end it, and which other Pacific island states still have security arrangements with Beijing?

2. Iraq's central government just took control of the Kurdish region's oil fields and borders, ending the most valuable part of a 30-year autonomy.

The background you need: The Kurds are an ethnic group of roughly 30–40 million spread across Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Syria, with no country of their own. Iraqi Kurdistan, in the north, has run itself since 1991 and was recognized as an autonomous region in Iraq's 2005 constitution. Its independence rested on two things: selling its own oil and controlling its own border crossings, mainly with Turkey. Baghdad has spent years pushing back through the courts, and in 2022 Iraq's top court struck down the region's oil law.

What happened: Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi announced he was placing the Kurdistan Region's oilfields and border crossings under federal control, citing oil smuggling (Nov 24). The next day the Federal Supreme Court ruled that the federal government may deploy troops to the region's borders and oversee and audit its oil industry (Nov 25). In the same week Turkey reported that the PKK, the Kurdish militant group based in Kurdistan's mountains, had evacuated 14 more bases in northern Iraq as part of its disarmament (Nov 23).

Why you'll care later: Control of oil revenue and borders is the core of statehood. If the takeover holds, Iraqi Kurdistan shrinks from a near-state to an ordinary province, and Iraq becomes considerably more centralized now that US forces have withdrawn. Where this lands decides the balance of power inside Iraq, and how well a federal model can survive elsewhere in the Middle East (Syria is the obvious next test).

Thread to pull: How did the Kurdistan Regional Government respond to Baghdad's takeover of its oilfields and border crossings in November 2026, and does it leave the region with any independent revenue?

3. The UN climate talks just ended with no agreement at all, after the longest summit on record.

The background you need: Each year almost every country meets at a UN climate conference, called a "COP," to negotiate how fast the world moves away from the fossil fuels that warm the planet. Decisions need consensus, so a single country can block the outcome. Even so, these summits nearly always end with some agreed text, however weak. The last comparable breakdown was at The Hague in 2000.

What happened: COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, ended without a final agreement because countries couldn't reach consensus on fossil fuels (Nov 22). It ran longer than any climate summit before it. The same week, the UK Health Security Agency reported 5,258 heat-related deaths in the UK in 2026, the most on record, beating 2022 (Nov 24). Meanwhile the strongest El Niño in years is building toward its peak.

Why you'll care later: The COP process is the only forum where every country negotiates climate together. If its biggest question, fossil fuels, can no longer get past a consensus vote, climate policy will shift to clubs and unilateral action: carbon border taxes, bilateral deals, national industrial policy. Five years out, this summit may mark the moment that shift started.

Thread to pull: Which countries or blocs blocked the fossil-fuel language at COP31 in Antalya, what happens procedurally when a COP ends without a decision, and what does that mean for COP32?

AI & Tech

Washington's hand on AI compute

FCC clears SpaceX's "Starmind" orbital data centers. On Nov 25 the FCC conditionally authorized SpaceX to launch up to one million satellites for orbital AI compute. Milestones run to the 2030s, the debris-disposal waiver is deferred, and SpaceX's IPO filing targets first deployments as early as 2028. Amazon, Viasat and astronomers all opposed it. Why it matters to you: the bottleneck on frontier AI has moved from chips to power and grid hookups, and this is the first regulator-blessed bet on getting around both. Hardly any of it will exist this decade. What changes now is how compute supply gets priced and argued about, and SpaceX gets a strong card in its IPO story. Track it as a strategic signal, not a near-term capacity change. CNBC

Nvidia's board reinstates Jensen Huang, with Washington weighing in. Huang resigned on Nov 12. On Nov 23 the board voted unanimously to bring him back as CEO and director, after "input from senior levels of the United States government" (AP reports Trump pushed for it). A week earlier the board had said it wouldn't. The company also says Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Why it matters to you: the leadership of the most important AI supply-chain company is now openly treated as a national-security matter. Add this to the export controls and the SpaceX ruling above: the US state now has a direct hand in who runs AI compute and where it gets built. Expect lab and chip strategy to follow Washington's priorities more closely from here. For the deeper read, Axios covers the governance risk of a board reversing itself under government pressure, which is the part that will outlast the headline; Ben Thompson's "Obituaries" (Reading pile) covers what the resignation revealed. Nvidia release, Axios

Agents get the keys

Anthropic now lets an AI approve routine code changes with no human sign-off. Claude Ship is three agents that take a GitHub pull request from review to approval: Code Review flags bugs (live for everyone), PR Steward (beta) pushes fixes for failing checks, merge conflicts and reviewer comments, and Claude Approvals (beta) approves routine PRs that pass a policy file you write in the repo. Typing /ship in Claude Code commits, opens the PR and watches it for up to 7 days. Your branch rules still decide what actually merges, and Team/Enterprise admins can switch it on for every PR. (AlphaSignal)

AI agents can now put temporary shields on live Linux servers while the real security fix is being written. Cisco and OpenAI released Shirudo, a free, open-source tool that blocks the exact path an attacker would use against a known vulnerability, without restarting the machine. Agents can scan for exposed systems, generate shields, test them against real exploits, and roll them out across a fleet. It doesn't replace patches; it covers the gap until they ship. (AlphaSignal)

Claude becomes a platform

Anthropic is turning Claude from a chat window into a place where apps live. A new "Install as app" beta turns any Claude artifact into a Mac or Windows desktop app with notifications, keyboard shortcuts, a menu-bar presence and local file access. The catches: apps need a signed-in Claude session and an internet connection to open, permissions reset on every update, and Anthropic doesn't review apps, so vetting them is on you. Separately, MCP (the standard for plugging tools into AI) gained Skills that ship instructions alongside tools, background Tasks, mid-workflow approval prompts before spending money or deleting data, and direct file uploads. A Claude plugins launch also brings 10,000+ tools to every plan. (AlphaSignal)

Safety under pressure

OpenAI says people are cloning its models to strip out the safety rules, and it's now banning thousands of accounts a week over it. In "adversarial distillation," someone trains a cheap copy on a strong model's outputs, keeping the capabilities and losing the refusals. OpenAI says its layered defenses (training, classifiers, API restrictions) cut attack success from 54% on GPT-6 Astra to 0.2% on GPT-6.2 Astra. Weekly enforcement hits thousands to tens of thousands of accounts, and older model families with weaker safeguards can no longer read GPT-6.2's protected reasoning, a sign of how AI APIs will get locked down from here. (AlphaSignal)

AI in the drug lab

An OpenAI model designed proteins that actually stuck to drug targets in real lab tests, not just simulations. Working with a biotech partner, GPT-6.2 did the whole design job through plain prompts and Python it wrote itself, replacing a stack of specialized tools. 35 of 322 candidates bound their targets across five proteins, including the cancer-immunotherapy target PD-L1, and 14 of 107 designed from scratch also bound. Caveats: its full structure prediction still trails specialist tools, binding in a dish isn't a working drug, and independent review is pending. (AlphaSignal)

A separate OpenAI benchmark shows where AI chemistry still falls short: picking which compounds to test next. MedChemBench draws 99 problems from 117 papers and patents. GPT-6 Astra scored 95.4% at reading molecular structures but only about half on choosing candidates and predicting how drugs behave. OpenAI frames it as a capability signal, not proof AI can find viable drugs on its own. (AlphaSignal)

Under the hood

A 70-year-old algorithm at the core of AI search just got a modern rewrite, making it cheap enough to rebuild search indexes nightly instead of every few months. Meta FAIR's e-means replaces the classic k-means clustering that vector databases use to group documents. Across 24 benchmarks it beats the standard method in about a tenth of the passes and runs 10–45x faster on GPUs, with no per-dataset tuning and almost no wasted empty clusters. It's in Faiss 1.16.0 under the MIT license. Self-hosted teams can try it now; users of managed vector databases like Pinecone can't, because the clustering is hidden inside the product. (AlphaSignal)

Operating Advice

One CTO's AI chief of staff approves travel, orders lunch and phones coworkers, and the hard part was writing its personality, not wiring up its tools. Innovaccer's Ankit Maheshwari runs "Jarvis" on OpenClaw on a Mac Mini, starting with a cron job that briefs him every four hours on resumes awaiting approval. Splitting voice calls off to a dedicated agent fixed persona drift, and an episode where it approved expiring travel requests while he was in transit (sensibly, as it happened) showed him which other actions needed hard limits. It costs about $500–600 a month on Opus 5.5, and he's testing near-free local models for the routine work (writeup, approval-digest workflow, specialist-agents workflow). (How I AI / Lenny's Newsletter)
