Weekly Digest — Nov 14 – Nov 20, 2026

The World Court ruled that Myanmar committed genocide, the leaders of the US, China and Russia met as a trio for the first time, and the US lost its measles-free status. In AI, models started redesigning the chips they run on, OpenAI released a model it rates as able to speed up AI research itself, and Anthropic filed to go public.

The World

The world's top court just ruled that Myanmar committed genocide. It's the first time the court has found a country directly guilty of it.

The background you need: Myanmar (formerly Burma) is a Southeast Asian country between India and Thailand. The Rohingya are a mostly Muslim minority in its western Rakhine state, and Myanmar has long refused them citizenship. In 2017 the military drove them out with mass killings, rapes and village burnings, and more than 700,000 fled to refugee camps in Bangladesh, where most still live. In 2019 The Gambia, a small West African country acting for the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, sued Myanmar at the International Court of Justice (ICJ) in The Hague. The ICJ is the UN's court for disputes between states.

What happened: On Nov 19, after seven years of proceedings, the ICJ ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya. The court's only earlier genocide case on the merits, Bosnia v. Serbia (2007), found that Serbia had failed to prevent the Srebrenica genocide. It stopped short of finding that Serbia itself committed genocide. This ruling goes further.

Why you'll care later: This is a landmark in international law. It is the first time a state has been found legally responsible for genocide, not just individuals tried at tribunals. Pending cases will be argued against it, including South Africa's genocide case against Israel over Gaza and Ukraine's case against Russia. It also sets the official record on what happened to the Rohingya, which matters for reparations, refugee returns, and any future settlement in Myanmar. The court has no army to enforce rulings. Whether this one changes anything on the ground will show how much international courts can do.

Thread to pull: What remedies can the ICJ order after a genocide finding, how could they be enforced against Myanmar's military government, and how does this ruling bear on the pending South Africa v. Israel case?

The leaders of the US, China and Russia sat down together for the first time and signed a joint statement on nuclear war, oil and UN reform.

The background you need: The US, China and Russia are the three great military powers and all hold nuclear weapons. Each also has a veto on the UN Security Council. Leaders have met in pairs many times, but the three have never held a summit as a trio. APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) is an annual meeting of Pacific-rim leaders. China hosted it this year in Shenzhen, the tech-manufacturing city next to Hong Kong.

What happened: On Nov 19 in Shenzhen, Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin held their first trilateral meeting. They issued a joint declaration covering nuclear war, petroleum production and reform of the United Nations. The declaration's detailed commitments haven't been reported yet. The meeting came as the US is fighting a war with Iran, Russia's war in Ukraine continues, and US-mediated Russia–Ukraine talks are underway in Abu Dhabi.

Why you'll care later: The talking points matter less than the fact that the meeting happened at all. The three powers are acting as a club that settles big questions among themselves: nuclear risk, energy supply, and who runs the UN. That looks more like a 19th-century "concert of powers" than the US-led alliance system of the past 75 years. European allies, Ukraine and the Gulf states were not in the room. If later summits follow, historians may count this one as the start of the format. If the declaration turns out to be empty, it becomes a footnote. Either way it is the first meeting to compare later ones against.

Thread to pull: What exactly did the Shenzhen trilateral declaration commit the US, China and Russia to on nuclear weapons, oil output and UN reform, and how did European and Ukrainian governments react?

The US is officially no longer "measles-free," 26 years after it wiped the disease out.

The background you need: Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known and can kill children or leave them disabled. The vaccine nearly eliminated it, and in 2000 the US was declared measles-free, meaning the virus no longer spread continuously within the country. Cases since then mostly came from travellers. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the World Health Organization's office for the Americas, certifies this status. A country loses it after 12 straight months of local transmission. US vaccination rates have fallen in recent years, and vaccine-skeptical voices now shape federal health policy.

What happened: On Nov 17 PAHO revoked the measles-elimination status of the United States, Mexico and Bolivia. For the US it cited more than 12 months of continuous domestic spread, with more than 5,700 reported infections and multiple deaths in 2026.

Why you'll care later: This is a slow trend turning into an official status change. A disease the US had beaten is spreading locally again, the clearest public-health measure of falling trust in vaccines and in federal health agencies. Getting the status back takes at least a year with no local spread, which requires raising vaccination rates nationally. Expect it to come up in debates over federal health leadership, school vaccine mandates, and whether other once-beaten diseases (polio, rubella) return.

Thread to pull: What drove US measles vaccination rates down after 2019, which states account for most of the 2026 cases, and what would the US have to do to regain elimination status?

AI & Tech

AI building AI

AI is now redesigning the chips that AI runs on, and the changes are already headed to factories. Working with AMD, OpenAI's models rewrote chip circuit code and found up to 14% better power, performance and area on AMD's flagship GPU, roughly a year of normal progress in under five months. AMD has already put some of the changes into a design sent for manufacturing, and OpenAI saw gains on real silicon for its own AI chip, Jalapeño. It's also releasing GPT-Synopsys, a model trained to drive industry-standard chip design software, so teams outside the big labs can do the same. (AlphaSignal)

For the first time, OpenAI has publicly released a model that it rates as able to meaningfully speed up AI research itself. GPT-6.2 Astra is the first externally released model to get a "High" rating for AI self-improvement under OpenAI's own risk scale. It also jumped 22 points on cybersecurity benchmarks. Read it alongside last week's METR audit of Anthropic: two labs now say, in different ways, that the research loop is starting to feed itself. Pricing is in Quick hits. (AlphaSignal)

Who checks the labs

OpenAI let four independent safety teams inside its models, though one says it still can't see enough. In these "embedded assessments," outside experts get access to internal systems rather than just the public API. Apollo Research checks whether models could quietly work against their own oversight, METR digs into OpenAI's largest reinforcement-learning run for missed bad behavior, Transluce audits how good OpenAI's safety tests are, and Goodfire looks at model internals during training. Apollo says its access still doesn't meet its bar for a full evaluation. It's a real step toward third-party accountability, but not there yet. (AlphaSignal)

Anthropic is going public at a valuation of up to $1.45 trillion. It filed with the SEC to list on Nasdaq as ANT, offering 825 million shares at $70–78. Revenue for the first nine months of 2026 was $33.2 billion, up more than 1,200% year over year. That moves the safety-first lab into quarterly earnings scrutiny. For background on the person at the top, see the NYT profile of Dario Amodei in the Reading pile below. (AlphaSignal)

How agents should actually work

AI agents do as well or better when they forget most of what they've done, as long as they keep a good briefing. Nvidia tested 13 benchmarks with agents that periodically compress their history into a short state summary and start fresh. Accuracy held up or improved: Claude Opus 4.5 went from 70% to 74.8% on a standard coding benchmark. For long documents, storing them as files the agent searches beat pasting them into the prompt. Two caveats. Some models collapse under tight limits (Qwen 3 Coder Next fell from 53% to 3.6% because its verbose output filled the budget), and compression breaks prompt caching, so a task can cost more despite using fewer tokens. A good summary keeps the goal, known facts, what failed and the next step, not the most memorable events. (AlphaSignal)

Coding agents now have AI managers. Cognition's Devin Bots take a big goal, split it into tasks, run several Devin agents in parallel, check their work, and only ping a human when a real decision is needed. Bots hand work to each other (a QA bot passes a bug to a developer bot) and keep memory across days or weeks. Beta testers merge 4.5x more pull requests per message, and one team went from 50 to nearly 2,000 PRs a week, which raises the obvious question of who reviews them all. (AlphaSignal)

Operating Advice

Token fraud is quietly draining AI startups, and it pushed one founder to kill free trials altogether. Claire Vo says abuse cost ChatPRD tens of thousands of dollars, even with SOC 2 compliance and regular penetration testing. Fraudsters farmed trials to run expensive models for unrelated work, resold about half of her partner promo codes, and turned her custom MCP support into a 24/7 compute worker. Her fixes were blunt: no free trials or promo codes, country blocking, a cheap model that sorts suspicious sessions in real time, and a support agent that's barred from restoring banned accounts. Her rule: if you sell AI, you're selling compute, so track dollars rather than requests (writeup, bot-detection workflow). (How I AI / Lenny's Newsletter)

A math teacher built a shared "household brain" with Claude Code and Obsidian, and the key move was swapping long chats for files. Scott Farrar keeps context in Markdown files he can inspect and correct, so memory doesn't drift inside an opaque conversation. Claude suggested photographing every appliance, which became a searchable inventory of model numbers and warranties. It also compares insurance quotes against the house's details and keeps one master to-do list, with a custom linter to stop agents bloating it. The family compromise: GitHub for shared scripts, Dropbox for everyday files, plus a wall display so the whole household actually uses it (writeup, knowledge-base workflow, wall dashboard). (How I AI / Lenny's Newsletter)
